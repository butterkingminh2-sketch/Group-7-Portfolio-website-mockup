I came to UX through frustration — specifically, the frustration of using digital products that clearly had not been designed with the user in mind. As someone who had grown up watching my parents struggle with poorly designed banking apps and government service portals, I became convinced early on that design is not an aesthetic choice but a moral one. Every unnecessary click, every confusing label, every hidden button represents a real cost to a real person.

As a third-year Business Administration student, I have focused my electives and independent projects on human-centred design and service innovation. My most impactful project was a UX overhaul of the checkout experience for a Vietnamese fashion e-commerce brand. Working with a team of four, I conducted 18 usability interviews, synthesised pain points using affinity mapping, and prototyped three alternative checkout flows in Figma. The winning design, validated through A/B testing, reduced cart abandonment by 22% — a result that translated directly into measurable revenue impact.

My second major project brought design thinking into a purely academic context. I led a UX audit of our university's student portal, recruiting 30 participants across different year groups and faculties. The audit combined heuristic evaluation with moderated usability sessions, producing a prioritised roadmap of 14 interface improvements. Three of those improvements were implemented by the IT department within the semester.

What excites me most about UX as a discipline is that it refuses to let business strategy hide behind jargon. When you put a prototype in front of a real person, the feedback is immediate, unambiguous, and humbling. I find that quality of truth-telling essential — and I try to bring it into every project, designed or otherwise.
