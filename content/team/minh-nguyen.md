Numbers have always felt honest to me in a way that words sometimes do not. A well-built financial model cannot hide its assumptions — every input is visible, every output traceable. That transparency is what drew me to finance, and what continues to drive how I approach every project: make the reasoning explicit, make the assumptions defensible, make the conclusion clear.

I am a third-year Business Administration student specialising in corporate finance and investment analysis. My academic work has centred on valuation methodology, particularly the challenge of applying standard frameworks to businesses that operate outside the textbook assumptions — small firms, high-growth sectors, and markets with limited comparable data. My most referenced project was a valuation framework I built for Vietnamese manufacturing SMEs, which replaced several ad hoc appraisal methods used informally by two faculty research groups. They now use it as a starting template for student case competitions.

My second major project moved me into the equity markets. I constructed and backtested a four-asset portfolio strategy using Vietnamese equities, working with 24 months of historical data and Python for data processing. The strategy outperformed its benchmark on a risk-adjusted basis, though I learned as much from the months it underperformed as from the ones it did not. Understanding why a model fails is, I think, the most valuable thing a financial analyst can practise.

Outside of coursework, I am working through the CFA Level I curriculum independently — not because the credential is required, but because the discipline of studying it forces a rigour that university examinations alone do not always demand. I find that the hardest questions in finance are rarely about the maths. They are about knowing which question to ask in the first place.
