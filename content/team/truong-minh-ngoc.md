Good operations are invisible — when they work perfectly, nobody notices. When they fail, everyone does. This asymmetry is what makes operations management both challenging and deeply satisfying. I am drawn to the discipline because it demands the kind of rigorous, unglamorous thinking that ultimately determines whether an organisation's strategy succeeds in practice or fails at the point of execution.

At HSB University, I concentrate on understanding how organisations design and maintain efficient processes. My most substantial practical experience came from coordinating the operations of a large faculty business forum. With 300 attendees, 12 guest speakers, and multiple concurrent sessions, the margin for error was minimal. The experience taught me that good coordination is mostly about clear information — who needs to know what, by when, and in what format.

I approach work with a preference for documentation and checklists. I find that making implicit knowledge explicit — writing things down, drawing process maps, building simple trackers — is one of the highest-value activities a team can invest in early in a project.

Outside of formal coursework, I am interested in supply chain fundamentals and how logistics decisions at the operational level affect a company's strategic flexibility. I believe that understanding the physical movement of goods and information is essential context for any business administrator who wants to make decisions that hold up in the real world.
